Fall 2026: Math 590 Quiz 10 Solutions

Problem 1

State the Singular Value Decomposition theorem. (3 points)

Solution. Singular Value Decomposition theorem. Let \(A\) be an \(m\times n\) matrix with entries in \(\mathbb{R}\) and rank \(r\). Then there exist an \(m\times m\) orthogonal matrix \(Q\), an \(n\times n\) orthogonal matrix \(P\), and an \(m\times n\) matrix

\[\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix} \sigma_1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 & 0 & \cdots & 0\\ 0 & \sigma_2 & \cdots & 0 & 0 & \cdots & 0\\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots & \vdots & & \vdots\\ 0 & 0 & \cdots & \sigma_r & 0 & \cdots & 0\\ 0 & 0 & \cdots & 0 & 0 & \cdots & 0\\ \vdots & \vdots & & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots\\ 0 & 0 & \cdots & 0 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \sigma_1 \geq \sigma_2 \geq \cdots \geq \sigma_r > 0,\]

that is, the \((i,i)\) entry of \(\Sigma\) is \(\sigma_i\) for \(1 \leq i \leq r\), and every other entry of \(\Sigma\) is \(0\), such that

\[A = Q\Sigma P^t.\]

The numbers \(\sigma_1, \ldots, \sigma_r\) are the singular values of \(A\): \(\sigma_i = \sqrt{\lambda_i}\), where \(\lambda_1 \geq \cdots \geq \lambda_r > 0\) are the nonzero eigenvalues of \(A^tA\).

Problem 2

Find the singular value decomposition for the matrix \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2\\2 & 1\\2 & -2\end{pmatrix}\). (7 points)

Solution. Step 1: the eigenvalues of \(A^tA\). We have

\[A^tA = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 2\\ 2 & 1 & -2\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2\\2 & 1\\2 & -2\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 9 & 0\\ 0 & 9\end{pmatrix} = 9I,\]

so the eigenvalues of \(A^tA\) are \(\lambda_1 = \lambda_2 = 9\), and the singular values of \(A\) are \(\sigma_1 = \sigma_2 = 3\). Thus

\[\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 0\\ 0 & 3\\ 0 & 0\end{pmatrix}.\]

Step 2: the matrix \(P\). Since \(A^tA = 9I\), every nonzero vector in \(\mathbb{R}^2\) is an eigenvector of \(A^tA\), so any orthonormal basis of \(\mathbb{R}^2\) will do. We take \(p_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 1\\0\end{pmatrix}\), \(p_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 0\\1\end{pmatrix}\), so \(P = I\).

Step 3: the matrix \(Q\). The first two columns of \(Q\) are

\[q_1 = \frac{1}{\sigma_1}Ap_1 = \frac{1}{3}\begin{pmatrix} 1\\2\\2\end{pmatrix}, \qquad q_2 = \frac{1}{\sigma_2}Ap_2 = \frac{1}{3}\begin{pmatrix} 2\\1\\-2\end{pmatrix}.\]

These are orthonormal: \(q_1\cdot q_2 = \frac{1}{9}(2 + 2 - 4) = 0\) and \(\|q_1\| = \|q_2\| = \frac{1}{3}\sqrt{9} = 1\). To complete \(q_1, q_2\) to an orthonormal basis of \(\mathbb{R}^3\), we choose a vector not in \(\mathrm{Span}\{q_1, q_2\}\), say \(v_3 = \begin{pmatrix} 1\\0\\0\end{pmatrix}\), and use the Gram-Schmidt formula to find a vector orthogonal to \(q_1\) and \(q_2\):1

\[u_3 = v_3 - (v_3\cdot q_1)\,q_1 - (v_3\cdot q_2)\,q_2.\]

(Since \(q_1\) and \(q_2\) are unit vectors, the denominators \(q_1\cdot q_1\) and \(q_2\cdot q_2\) in the Gram-Schmidt formula are both \(1\).) Here \(v_3\cdot q_1 = \frac{1}{3}\) and \(v_3\cdot q_2 = \frac{2}{3}\), so

\[u_3 = \begin{pmatrix} 1\\0\\0\end{pmatrix} - \frac{1}{3}\cdot\frac{1}{3}\begin{pmatrix} 1\\2\\2\end{pmatrix} - \frac{2}{3}\cdot\frac{1}{3}\begin{pmatrix} 2\\1\\-2\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 - \frac{1}{9} - \frac{4}{9}\\[4pt] 0 - \frac{2}{9} - \frac{2}{9}\\[4pt] 0 - \frac{2}{9} + \frac{4}{9}\end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{9}\begin{pmatrix} 4\\-4\\2\end{pmatrix}.\]

Check: \(u_3\cdot q_1 = \frac{1}{27}(4 - 8 + 4) = 0\) and \(u_3\cdot q_2 = \frac{1}{27}(8 - 4 - 4) = 0\). Since \(\|u_3\| = \frac{1}{9}\sqrt{16 + 16 + 4} = \frac{6}{9} = \frac{2}{3}\), normalizing gives

\[q_3 = \frac{u_3}{\|u_3\|} = \frac{3}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{9}\begin{pmatrix} 4\\-4\\2\end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{3}\begin{pmatrix} 2\\-2\\1\end{pmatrix}.\]

Therefore

\[A = Q\Sigma P^t, \qquad Q = \frac{1}{3}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 2\\ 2 & 1 & -2\\ 2 & -2 & 1\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \Sigma = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 0\\ 0 & 3\\ 0 & 0\end{pmatrix}, \qquad P = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0\\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}.\]

Check: \(Q\Sigma = \frac{1}{3}\begin{pmatrix} 3 & 6\\ 6 & 3\\ 6 & -6\end{pmatrix} = A\), and \(P^t = I\).

1 Alternatively, one can take the cross product \(q_1\times q_2 = \frac{1}{9}\begin{pmatrix} 1\\2\\2\end{pmatrix}\times\begin{pmatrix} 2\\1\\-2\end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{9}\begin{pmatrix} -6\\6\\-3\end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{3}\begin{pmatrix} -2\\2\\-1\end{pmatrix}\), which is orthogonal to both \(q_1\) and \(q_2\). Since \(q_1\) and \(q_2\) are orthonormal, \(q_1\times q_2\) is already a unit vector, so no normalization is needed. This is \(-q_3\), which works equally well as the third column of \(Q\). ↩
